# Voice clone reading script (about 30 minutes)

Purpose: recordings for the ElevenLabs Professional voice clone. Read in your normal speaking voice, as if explaining things to a friendly agent across a table. Do not perform and do not read in a flat, "radio" voice.

## Before you start

- Quiet room, soft furnishings if possible (a lounge or a wardrobe full of clothes beats a bathroom or kitchen). Turn off fans, air conditioning, the fridge-side door and notifications.
- Phone voice memo app (or QuickTime on your Mac), about 20 cm from your mouth, same distance throughout. Do not hold it in your hand and fiddle with it; prop it up.
- One recording per section (about 2 to 3 minutes each), so a mistake costs you minutes, not the whole session. Name the files section-01 to section-15.
- If you stumble, pause for a second, then go back to the start of that sentence and read it again. Do not stop the recording. You will not need to edit.
- Sip water. Aim for the same energy throughout. Where you see (smile) or (slower) it is a light nudge, not a command.
- Read the section name aloud at the start of each file only if you like; it is not needed.

## Section 01: Saturday morning

It's seven forty on a Saturday morning, and the open home starts at ten. The signs are going up, the flyers are in the folder, and the first coffee is already going cold. Every agent knows this feeling. There's a list in your head that never gets shorter. Unlock the property. Turn on the lights. Open the blinds. Check the air. Put the sign-in sheet on the bench, next to a pen that probably won't work.

By ten past ten, the first couple walk in. They smile, they look around, and they say very little. By half past ten, there are eleven people in the kitchen. Some of them are serious. Some are just curious. A few are there because it's Saturday and they like looking at other people's houses. And that's fine. That's part of the job.

But here's the problem. When everyone leaves, you have a pile of paper, a head full of impressions, and a seller who is going to ring you on Monday and ask a very simple question. How did it go?

You could say, "Good turnout." You could say, "People seemed keen." And honestly, most of the time that's what agents say. It isn't wrong. It just isn't much use to a seller who is making a decision about their biggest asset.

What if you could say something different? What if you could say, "Thirty-one people came through. Nine of them liked the kitchen. Four were put off by the road noise. And two of them told me they're ready to make an offer."

That's a different phone call. That's a different Monday. And it doesn't take a longer Saturday. It takes a smarter one.

Let me say that again, slowly. It doesn't take a longer Saturday. It takes a smarter one.

## Section 02: What buyers actually say

Buyers are honest, when you ask them in the right way. They are honest on their phone, in the car park, a few minutes after they've left. They are much less honest standing in your kitchen with the seller's family photos on the fridge.

Think about the last open home you ran. How many people told you the truth at the door? They said, "Lovely home, thanks so much." And they walked out. Then they went home and told their partner, "The second bedroom is tiny, and I don't like the street."

That information is gold. It tells you what to say to the seller. It tells you whether the price is realistic. It tells you which buyers to ring first, and which to leave alone.

So the question is simple. How do you get it? You make it easy. You make it quick. You make it private. And you make it so easy that people do it before they've even started the car.

A QR code on the flyer. A link in the online listing. A form that opens on their phone, with no app to download and no account to create. A few taps. Interest level. A star rating for price, for condition, for location. A line for their name, if they want to leave one. Done.

Now, I want to be careful here. I'm not saying every visitor will fill it in. They won't. People are busy, and some people simply don't want to. But a surprising number will, if it takes under a minute and asks for nothing awkward.

And here's the bit agents tend to miss. Even a fifty per cent response tells you far more than a handshake and a "see you later."

Say it with me, if you like. More answers. Fewer guesses.

## Section 03: Offers

Let's talk about offers, because offers are where the real pressure lives.

An offer can arrive at any time. It can arrive on the Saturday, in the driveway, on the back of a business card. It can arrive on Sunday night, by text message, from a buyer who has been thinking about it all day. It can arrive on Monday morning, when you're halfway through your second email.

And then there are two of them. Or three.

Now you're not just receiving offers. You're comparing them. One is higher, but subject to finance. One is lower, but cash with a short settlement. One is in the middle, and the buyer wants the curtains and the dishwasher included. Each of them is a different shape. Each of them needs to be laid next to the others, in a way your seller can understand.

That's hard to do when the offers live in different places. One in an email. One in a message. One scribbled on a piece of paper you can't quite read.

Here's what makes it easier. A single form. The buyer enters their name, their email, their offer amount, how they're financing the purchase, how long they'd like for settlement, and whether they'd be willing to waive the building and pest inspection. Everything arrives in one place, in the same format, every time.

And then, because you've told the system what the seller actually cares about, the offers can be lined up and ranked against it. Price first, if the seller wants price. Certainty first, if the seller wants certainty.

You still make the call. You always make the call. But now you're making it with the whole picture in front of you, and not with five tabs open and a headache.

## Section 04: Rentals

If you work in property management, you already know this. Rental applications are a different kind of busy.

A rental goes on the market on a Thursday. By Saturday, there are twenty groups through the door. Some of them fall in love at the front step. Some of them need to see the bathroom twice. Some of them bring their parents.

Everyone says they'll send an application. Not everyone does. And the ones who do send it, send it in every way imaginable. A photo of a form. A PDF. An email with half the details. A message that says, "Hi, I'm interested, what do you need?"

Then it's Sunday night. You're at the kitchen table, with a laptop, a cold cup of tea, and a spreadsheet that gets a little more tangled every time you add a row.

There is a much easier way to do it. The applicant scans the QR code, or taps the link, and the application opens on their phone. Their name. Their email. Where they work, what they do, roughly what the household earns, when they'd like to move in, and who can speak for them as a reference. They tick a box to say they can provide proof of income if asked. They tap submit. That's it.

Nothing to print. Nothing to scan. Nothing to download. And nothing for you to re-type.

Every application lands in the same place, sorted by property. On Monday morning, you open one list. You don't open nine email threads and a folder of photos.

It sounds small. But if you manage fifty properties, small turns into hours very quickly.

## Section 07: Longer, calmer reading

This section is slower and a little more reflective. Read it as though you're recording a podcast, with a cup of tea beside you.

I've often thought that the best agents are the ones who listen. Not the ones who talk the loudest, or the ones who have the biggest signboards. The ones who ask a good question, and then wait. They let the silence do some of the work. They notice the buyer who stands a little longer in the second bedroom. They notice the couple who keep looking at each other, and say nothing.

Listening is a skill. And like any skill, it gets sharper with practice, and it gets blunter when you're tired, or rushed, or carrying too much in your head.

That's where good tools help. A good tool doesn't replace the agent. It doesn't take over the relationship. It just takes the small, tedious things off the table, so that the agent can be fully present, at the moment it counts.

Think about what a seller really wants. They want to feel looked after. They want to know that someone is paying attention. They want to be told the truth, kindly, with evidence. And they want to feel that the person they chose is on top of it.

When you ring a seller on Monday and say, "Here's what thirty-one visitors told us," you aren't just giving them information. You're giving them confidence. You're showing them that nothing was left to chance. And that confidence is what wins the next listing, and the one after that.

It is quiet work. It rarely gets applauded. But it is the work that builds a reputation, one open home at a time.

## Section 14: Practical open-home tips

Read these like a friendly checklist you'd give a newer agent. Keep it relaxed and conversational.

Here are a few small habits that make an open home run more smoothly.

Arrive early. Thirty minutes is ideal. It gives you time to walk through the house, check each room, and fix the things that are slightly off. A crooked cushion. A dripping tap. A door that doesn't quite close.

Open every blind, and turn on every light. Even in the middle of the day. A bright house feels bigger and friendlier.

Put the flyers where people will see them. Not on the back bench, hidden behind the fruit bowl. Near the front door, at eye height, with the QR code clearly visible.

Greet everyone. Even the people who look like they're only passing through. A warm hello changes how people feel in the house.

Ask one good question. Not "Do you like it?" That gets a polite yes. Try, "What are you looking for in your next home?" That gets a real answer.

Don't rush the quiet ones. Some of your best buyers will say very little. Let them wander, and let them linger.

Keep an eye on the time. If the crowd is thin at the end, stay a few minutes longer. Latecomers are often the most serious.

Tidy up before you go. Close the windows, lock the doors, collect the flyers, and leave the place the way you found it.

And the next morning, follow up. Don't leave it for a week. Buyers cool off quickly, and sellers get nervous when they don't hear from you.

None of this is complicated. But when you do it consistently, it adds up to a reputation. People notice who's careful, and who's calm, and who always follows through.
